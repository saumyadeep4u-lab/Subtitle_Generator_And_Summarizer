Welcome to lecture 4 on computer vision and convolutional neural network architectures. Digital images are represented as three-dimensional tensors defined by height, width, and color channels such as red, green, and blue. Connecting a standard fully connected layer directly to a high resolution image requires hundreds of millions of parameters and destroys spatial topology. Convolutional neural networks, or CNNs, solve this problem through three architectural priors, local receptive fields, spatial weight sharing, and translation equivariants. A learnable convolutional filter, typically three by three or five by five pixels in size, slides across the spatial dimensions of the input tensor to compute feature maps. Because the exact same filter weights are shared across every spatial location, the network detects visual patterns regardless of where they appear in the frame. In section 2, we examine spatial dimensions, padding, stride, and pooling operations inside a convolutional backbone. Zero padding adds a border of zeros around the input perimeter so convolutional filters can process edge pixels without shrinking spatial resolution prematurely. Stride defines the step size by which the convolutional kernel shifts horizontally and vertically across the image grid. After convolution and batch normalization, nonlinear activation functions introduce sparsity into the resulting activation maps. Max pooling and average pooling downsample feature maps by aggregating local neighborhoods, reducing memory footprint and providing invariants to small spatial shifts. Early convolutional layers learn low-level edge corner and color gradient detectors, whereas deeper layers compose these primitives into textures, object parts, and complete semantic categories. In section 3, we study deep residual networks, transfer learning, and vision backbones. As early architectures scaled beyond 20 layers, researchers observed a counterintuitive degradation problem where training accuracy saturated and then worsened rapidly. ResNet resolved the spottle neck by introducing identity skip connections that add the input of a convolutional block directly to its output. Instead of learning an unreferenced target mapping from scratch, residual blocks learn a residual perturbation relative to the identity function. In real-world applications with limited labeled images, engineers initialize vision pipelines with ResNet or Vision transformer weights predrained on millions of labeled images. By freezing early feature extraction layers and fine-tuning only the final classification head with data augmentation, models converge rapidly with high accuracy. In section 4, we extend image classification to object detection and semantic segmentation. Whereas image classification assigns a single category label to an entire frame, object detection outputs both bounding box coordinates and class probabilities for every object present. Two stage detectors generate candidate region proposals before refining box regressions, while single stage detectors like Yolo predict bounding boxes and classes in a single forward pass. Non-maximum suppression filters out redundant overlapping bounding boxes by sorting predictions by confidence and discarding boxes whose intersection over union exceeds a configured threshold. Mean average precision across multiple intersection over union threshold serves as the standard benchmark metric for evaluating object detection systems. In section 5, we perform a concrete calculation of convolutional output dimensions and receptive field growth across stack layers. Given an input image of size 224 by 224 with 3 RGB channels, applying 64 convolutional filters of size 3 by 3 with a padding of 1 pixel and a stride of 1 preserves the exact spatial dimensions of 224 by 224 while producing 64 output feature channels. Each 3 by 3 filter contains 3 times 3 times 3 equals 27 weights plus 1 by a term, totaling just 1,792 parameters for the entire layer. Furthermore, stacking 2,3 by 3 convolutional layers back to back achieves the exact same 5 by 5 effective receptive field as a single 5 by 5 convolution, while requiring 28 percent fewer parameters and introducing an additional non-linear activation in between. In section 6, we examine how depthwise separable convolutions enable real-time computer vision on mobile and edge devices. Standard convolution performs spatial filtering and cross-channel feature combination simultaneously in a single heavy operation. MobileENet architectures factorize this operation into two lightweight steps. First, a depthwise convolution applies a single spatial filter independently to each input channel. Second, a pointwise 1 by 1 convolution projects and combines the channel outputs linearly. For a 3 by 3 kernel, depthwise separable convolution reduces floating point multiply accumulate operations by a factor of 8 to 9 times with only a minimal tradeoff in top 1 classification accuracy. In section 7, we explore encoder decoder segmentation architectures and feature pyramid networks for multi-scale visual recognition. Recognizing both tiny distant objects and large foreground objects requires combining high-resolution spatial detail from early layers with rich semantic context from deep layers. Unit and feature pyramid networks construct top-down lateral pathways that upsample deep semantic feature maps and fuse them elementwise with shallow high-resolution feature maps. To train pixelwise segmentation models underclass imbalance between foreground objects and background sky or road pixels, engineers combined cross-entropy loss with dice loss and focal loss. In our eighth and concluding section, we review advanced data augmentation paradigms and vision transformers. Beyond classical random horizontal flips, random resized crops, and color jittering, modern vision training pipelines incorporate mix-up and cut mix regularization. Mix-up forms synthetic training examples by taking convex linear combinations of pairs of images and their corresponding label vectors, while cut mix-paste rectangular patches across training images. Combined with vision transformers that partition images into 16 by 16 pixel patches processed via multi-head self-attention, modern computer vision systems achieve human level perception across medical imaging, autonomous navigation, and industrial inspection.
